Ideal Plot Expectations (Aerodynamic Reference)
When the propeller geometry is perfectly optimized for the design state (hover/climb), the resulting plots should exhibit the following ideal characteristics:

1. Angle of Attack (α) Distribution

Ideal Shape: A nearly flat, horizontal line across the entire span of the blade.

Why: The optimizer should try to keep the entire blade operating at the airfoil's most efficient lift-to-drag ratio (typically between 4° and 7°). A flat line proves the twist and chord are perfectly compensating for the changing inflow angles.

2. Chord Distribution & Blade Planform

Ideal Shape: The chord should start relatively narrow at the root cutout, rapidly expand to its maximum width (usually around 20% to 30% of the radius) to handle structural loads, and then smoothly and continuously taper down to a narrow point at the tip.

Why: A tapered tip minimizes high-speed tip drag, while a wider root provides necessary inner thrust and structural integrity. Wavy or expanding outer chords are physically inefficient.

3. Twist Distribution (β / Pitch)

Ideal Shape: A smooth, continuous curve that starts at its highest angle at the root and continuously decreases all the way to the tip (this is called "washout").

Why: The tip of the blade spins much faster through the air than the root. To maintain that flat Angle of Attack, the fast-moving tip needs a shallow pitch, while the slow-moving root needs a steep pitch to catch the air.

4. Inflow Angle Distribution (ϕ)

Ideal Shape: A smooth, swooping mathematical curve starting high at the root and curving downwards to a low angle at the tip.

Why: Rotational speed increases linearly with the radius. Because vertical climb speed is constant, the resulting angle of incoming air becomes much shallower as you move outward along the blade.

5. Radial Thrust Loading (dT/dr)

Ideal Shape: A smooth "bell curve" or semi-ellipse. It should be exactly zero at the root cutout (0.1R), smoothly rise to a peak around 70% to 80% of the radius, and then smoothly drop back down to exactly zero at the tip.

Why: This shows an even, efficient distribution of lift. If the curve has sharp spikes or valleys, it means tiny sections of the blade are doing all the work while the rest is dead weight. Tip loss physics dictate that thrust must drop to zero at the absolute tip.

6. Power vs. Climb Speed (Including & Excluding Climb)

Ideal Shape: Two smooth curves. As climb speed increases (+V), power increases.

Including Climb (Shaft Power): This represents the total electrical work the motor does to spin the blade and fight gravity. It will be the higher line during a climb.

Note: At extreme speeds (+10 m/s or -10 m/s), these lines may spike or break due to Fixed-Pitch Stall or Vortex Ring State (VRS), which are physical limitations of fixed-pitch propellers, not code bugs.

7. Trimmed RPM vs. Climb Speed

Ideal Shape: A smooth, upward-sloping line.

Why: As the drone climbs faster, the air rushes down through the disk faster, which lowers the Angle of Attack. The motor must spin faster to catch the air and maintain the target thrust.

8. Tip Mach vs. Climb Speed

Ideal Shape: Follows the exact same shape as the RPM curve.

Why: Tip Mach is directly proportional to RPM. Ideally, this line should stay safely below Mach 0.75 to avoid supersonic shockwaves, extreme drag, and noise.
